Notes and limitations

  • Source of the limits. The limits come from the CFIA page Preventive control recommendations on the use of nitrites in the curing of meat products (page dated 2021-10-06). Sodium nitrite is limited to 200 ppm (20 g per 100 kg), or 120 ppm for side bacon (12 g per 100 kg of pork bellies). In slow-cured products, sodium nitrate up to 200 ppm may be used in addition to nitrite. Dry rub cured products on racks may use up to 62 g of nitrite and 186 g of nitrate salts per 100 kg. To be called "cured", a product needs salt and at least 100 ppm of the nitrite or nitrate salts, singly or combined. Check the current CFIA page, since it may change.
  • What "ppm" means here. Levels are at the input stage: the nitrite or nitrate added, per kg of product. They are not residual levels in the finished product. 1 ppm = 1 mg/kg.
  • Weight basis. For batters, CFIA's example divides by the total batter weight including the cure. For injection, it divides by the weight after injection (green weight plus pump). The USDA handbook instead divides by the green weight before injection, so the same brine gives a higher ppm. The USDA limits also differ (for example 156 ppm for comminuted products). Results here use the CFIA basis. The USDA figure is shown only for comparison.
  • Brine and marinade soaks, and dry rubs. The CFIA page gives examples for batters and injection only. For soaks, this tool applies the same input-level idea and the USDA handbook's two immersion methods (pick-up, or equilibrium of meat and brine). For dry rubs it divides by the green weight of the meat. Confirm these choices with CFIA or your food safety professional.
  • Potassium salts. The limits are for sodium salts. Potassium nitrite and nitrate are converted to sodium equivalents by molar mass (81.07% and 84.07%), which is how the USDA handbook treats potassium nitrite.
  • Celery and vegetable powders. Enter the supplier's tested nitrite (cultured powder) or nitrate (non-converted powder) percentage. CFIA says the level of preformed nitrite must be known, the powder must be validated, and non-converted nitrate powders with a starter culture are only for emulsified red meat products, not poultry and not injection.
  • Safety. Pure sodium nitrite and nitrate are dangerous in small amounts. Weigh them with a suitable scale, keep bulk salts secure, and keep a record log of their use, as CFIA advises. Premixed cure mixes are safer to handle.
  • Not a validation. This is a learning tool. Real formulations must be checked against current regulations by a qualified professional.
